When the Water Rises: Uncovering the Environmental and Social Realities of Flooding in Intramuros, Manila

Behind the stone walls and heritage structures of the historic district lies a recurring challenge: urban flooding that touches residents, workers, students, vendors and visitors.

By Cherlize Lei T. Luzuriaga, Nina Jane Allysa S. Reyes & Chelsey Andrea V. Taruc · JENN35A · Adviser: Ms. Aurajue Bien K. Caparas ·

Intramuros is widely recognized for its historical landmarks, centuries-old architecture, and cultural significance. However, behind its stone walls and heritage structures lies an environmental challenge that continues to affect the people who inhabit, visit, and work within the historic district: urban flooding.

During periods of intense rainfall, streets and surrounding areas can become difficult to navigate as water accumulates faster than drainage systems can accommodate it. What may initially appear to be a temporary consequence of bad weather can develop into a recurring disruption, affecting transportation, education, employment, public safety, and everyday activities.

Flooding in Intramuros should not be viewed solely as a consequence of heavy rainfall. It is also connected to broader environmental concerns, including urban development, land-use conditions, drainage limitations, waste management, and the increasing risks associated with climate change.

An Environmental Problem Rooted in Urban Conditions

As cities expand, open spaces and natural surfaces that previously absorbed rainwater are replaced by buildings, roads, sidewalks, and other structures. These developments reduce the amount of water that can naturally infiltrate the ground, increasing surface runoff during rainfall. This is particularly relevant to Intramuros, where a dense concentration of buildings and paved surfaces creates challenges for managing rainwater.

Explore the factors

According to veteran architect and urban planner Felino “Jun” Palafox, flooding in Metro Manila results from several interconnected factors, including urbanization, inadequate planning, climate change, and improper waste disposal. His observations emphasize that flooding cannot be addressed through isolated solutions.

Data: Flood Control Spending

During the first three years of Ferdinand “Bongbong” Marcos’s presidency, the administration funded over 9,855 flood control projects worth over P545 billion between July 2022 and May 2025 (Latoza, 2025). Across the whole nation, Metro Manila received the largest share, with over P52.5 billion.

These conditions demonstrate that flooding is not simply a matter of water entering the streets. It reflects the relationship between natural processes and human activities that influence how water moves through an urban environment.

When Floodwater Becomes an Everyday Burden

Flooding is more than an environmental implication; it is a social challenge that affects Filipinos’ daily lives. For residents, workers, students, vendors, and tourists, flooded streets can turn ordinary routines into difficult and sometimes unsafe journeys. Urban flooding in Metro Manila is identified as a cause of traffic disruption, with flooded roads creating challenges beyond the presence of water itself (Mamuyac et al., 2023).

Commuters often experience delays, motorists struggle to pass, and pedestrians are forced to walk through water of uncertain depth and cleanliness. For small business owners, reduced foot traffic can affect daily income.

“Of course, it’s scary because the flood is deep and the wind is strong. That’s what affected me. I didn’t have an umbrella; I didn’t have a car.”— Baby Rose, parking attendant in Intramuros

This reflects how unsolved flooding makes workers vulnerable, especially those with limited means of protection or alternative transportation.

The Need for Sustainable and Long-Term Solutions

Previous flood-control initiatives in Intramuros have included drainage improvement projects, raised road infrastructure, and regular community clean-up campaigns (Ong, 2025). However, continued flooding suggests existing measures may still face limitations (Tadios, 2022). Sustainable urban planning, proper solid waste management, and climate adaptation must work together.

Who can do what?

In the end, this predicament paints a small portion of the larger picture. Continuous flash floods in highly urbanized cities, combined with repeated delayed class suspensions, underscore a collective struggle among students, professors, employees, and ordinary citizens.
